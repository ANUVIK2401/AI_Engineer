9 topics ~60 min deep-read Breadth [FREQUENTLY ASKED]

Interviewers ask about this because most real ML work has no labels and no LLM in it — clustering, anomaly detection, imbalance, and feature engineering are where projects actually succeed or quietly fail, and a GenAI-only answer here reveals a narrow foundation.

Clustering: K-Means, DBSCAN & Hierarchical

[CRITICAL]
  • K-means alternates assigning points to the nearest of k centroids and recomputing those centroids, minimizing within-cluster variance; DBSCAN grows clusters from dense regions defined by a radius (ε) and a minimum neighbor count, labeling sparse points as noise; hierarchical clustering builds a merge tree you can cut at any level.
  • They matter because unlabeled grouping is the most common form of exploratory analysis — customer segmentation, deduplication, log grouping, and the clustering stage of modern topic modeling all run on these.
  • The insight: k-means minimizes variance, which means it implicitly assumes clusters are spherical, similarly sized, and equally dense. Feed it two concentric rings and it will confidently split them down the middle — not because it failed, but because it optimized exactly what you asked. DBSCAN handles arbitrary shapes and rejects outliers, but degrades badly in high dimensions where distances concentrate and a single ε stops separating anything.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"How do you choose k for k-means?"
"I'd use the elbow method and pick where the curve bends."
The elbow method is a starting point, but on real data the curve is usually smooth with no clear elbow, so it's often a Rorschach test. I'd combine silhouette score, which measures how much better each point fits its own cluster than the next-nearest one and has an actual optimum rather than a monotone curve, with gap statistic if I want a principled comparison against a null reference distribution. But the more important point is that k is usually a business decision, not a statistical one: if marketing can run four campaigns, k=4 is the answer regardless of what the silhouette says. I'd also sanity-check stability — refit on bootstrap samples and see whether the same structure appears, because a k that changes cluster membership wildly across resamples isn't describing anything real.
Follow-up: "Why does k-means fail on concentric circles?"

PCA, t-SNE & UMAP

[TRADEOFF]
  • PCA finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance via eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix — a linear projection that is deterministic, invertible, and preserves global structure; t-SNE and UMAP are nonlinear methods that preserve local neighborhoods, optimizing so that nearby points in high dimensions stay nearby in 2D.
  • They matter for compressing features before modeling, for reducing embedding dimensionality to cut vector-DB cost and latency, and for the visual sanity checks that catch data problems no metric surfaces.
  • The insight: in a t-SNE or UMAP plot, cluster sizes and the distances between clusters are not meaningful. The algorithms optimize local neighborhood preservation and explicitly sacrifice global geometry — two clusters drawn far apart may be adjacent in the original space. People make architecture decisions from these plots constantly, and it's one of the most common analysis errors in ML.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Your embeddings are 1536-dimensional and the vector DB is too expensive. What are your options?"
"Run PCA down to 128 dimensions."
There are three levers and I'd try them in cost order. First, check whether the embedding model supports Matryoshka representation learning — models trained that way let you simply truncate the vector to a prefix with graceful quality loss, which costs nothing to implement. Second, quantize: scalar quantization to int8 cuts memory 4× with typically under 1% recall loss, and binary quantization cuts it 32× with a rescoring pass over the top candidates to recover precision — this is usually the best ratio of savings to effort. Third, PCA or a learned projection, fit on a representative sample of the corpus, which works but requires re-fitting when the corpus distribution shifts and makes the index non-portable. Whichever I pick, I'd measure recall@k against the full-precision index on real queries before and after, because the only number that matters is retrieval quality at the top of the ranking, not reconstruction error.
Follow-up: "How does binary quantization still retrieve accurately?"

Bagging, Boosting & Gradient Boosted Trees

[CRITICAL]
  • Bagging trains many models in parallel on bootstrap samples and averages them, reducing variance (random forests add feature subsampling at each split); boosting trains models sequentially where each new model fits the residual errors of the ensemble so far, reducing bias; XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are gradient-boosting implementations differing mainly in split-finding strategy and categorical handling.
  • They matter because gradient-boosted trees remain the strongest default for tabular data — they beat deep learning on most structured business problems, which is still the majority of production ML.
  • The insight: bagging and boosting attack opposite halves of the error decomposition. Bagging averages away variance and therefore cannot overfit much by adding trees — more trees only stabilize the estimate. Boosting reduces bias by fitting residuals, so more rounds keep lowering training error and it will overfit without early stopping. That single asymmetry explains why random forests are nearly hyperparameter-free while GBMs need careful tuning.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"When would you use a neural network instead of XGBoost on tabular data?"
"Neural networks are more powerful, so for any large dataset."
Rarely, and only for specific structural reasons rather than raw capacity. Gradient-boosted trees handle heterogeneous feature scales, missing values, and non-smooth decision boundaries natively, and they're far cheaper to train and tune — the published benchmarks consistently show them matching or beating deep models on typical tabular sizes. I'd reach for a neural network when the tabular data has structure trees can't exploit: high-cardinality categoricals where a learned embedding beats one-hot or target encoding, multi-modal inputs where I need to fuse text or image features with tabular ones in one model, multi-task setups sharing a representation across objectives, or when I need the model to be fine-tunable end-to-end as part of a larger system. If I just have a wide CSV and a target column, XGBoost or LightGBM is the right call and the deep model is usually engineering theater.

Feature Engineering & Encoding

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • Core moves: scaling (standardization vs min-max vs robust), categorical encoding (one-hot for low cardinality, target/mean encoding for high cardinality, learned embeddings for very high), binning continuous variables, interaction and ratio features, and cyclical encoding of time via sine/cosine pairs so 23:00 and 01:00 are close.
  • It matters because on tabular problems feature quality dominates model choice — a well-engineered feature set with logistic regression routinely beats raw features with a tuned GBM, and it's cheaper to maintain.
  • The insight: target encoding is the highest-leverage and most dangerous transform in the toolkit. Computing a category's mean target using rows that include the row you're encoding leaks the label directly into the feature — validation looks superb and production collapses. It must be computed out-of-fold, within the cross-validation loop, with smoothing toward the global mean for rare categories.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Your model scores 0.94 AUC offline and 0.71 in production. Where do you look first?"
"The production data must have drifted from the training distribution."
Drift is possible but it's rarely that large that fast, so I'd start with leakage, which produces exactly this signature. Three specific checks in order. First, temporal leakage: was the split random when it should have been chronological, and does any aggregate feature compute over a window that includes data after the prediction timestamp? Second, target leakage: is there a feature that is a downstream consequence of the label rather than an antecedent — a status field written after the outcome is known is the classic one. Third, preprocessing leakage: were the scaler, imputer, or target encoding fit on the full dataset instead of inside the CV folds. I'd also verify that the production feature pipeline computes each feature identically to training — training/serving skew from two separate implementations of the same feature is the other common cause, and it's why feature stores exist.
Follow-up: "How would you detect temporal leakage automatically?"

Imbalanced Data & Threshold Selection

[CRITICAL]
  • Options for class imbalance: resample (random undersampling of the majority, SMOTE synthetic oversampling of the minority), reweight the loss by inverse class frequency, or leave the data alone and move the decision threshold — plus evaluate with PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC, since ROC is optimistic under heavy imbalance.
  • It matters because the high-value ML problems are nearly all imbalanced: fraud, churn, defect detection, medical screening, incident prediction.
  • The insight: a well-calibrated model on imbalanced data is usually not broken — the default 0.5 threshold is. Resampling changes the base rate the model learns, so its outputs stop being calibrated probabilities and you've traded a threshold problem for a calibration problem. Tune the threshold on a validation set against the actual cost matrix first; reach for resampling only when the minority class is so rare the model has too few gradient signals to learn anything at all.
▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Fraud is 0.3% of transactions. Walk me through how you'd build and evaluate the model."
"I'd use SMOTE to balance the classes and then optimize F1."
I'd start without resampling: train a gradient-boosted model with class weights or scale_pos_weight, keep the outputs calibrated, and evaluate with PR-AUC because ROC-AUC is misleadingly high when true negatives dominate. Then the real work is the threshold. A missed fraud costs the chargeback amount; a false positive costs a blocked legitimate customer, which has a churn cost the business can estimate. I'd sweep the threshold on a chronologically held-out validation set to minimize expected cost, not maximize F1 — F1 implicitly asserts precision and recall are equally valuable, which is almost never true. I'd split evaluation by transaction value too, since the cost matrix isn't uniform. On SMOTE specifically: it interpolates between minority points, which in high dimensions can synthesize instances that don't lie on the real data manifold, and it destroys calibration. I'd only try it if the model was failing to learn the minority class at all, and I'd verify it helped on a real holdout rather than assuming.

Anomaly & Outlier Detection

[TRADEOFF]
  • Main families: statistical (z-score, IQR, distribution fitting), distance and density based (kNN distance, Local Outlier Factor), isolation-based (Isolation Forest, which exploits the fact that anomalies need fewer random splits to isolate), and reconstruction-based (autoencoder reconstruction error, PCA residual).
  • It matters for fraud, intrusion detection, manufacturing defects, and — increasingly — for detecting distribution drift and out-of-distribution inputs on a deployed model.
  • The insight: anomaly detection is not classification with a rare class. You typically have no labeled anomalies, the anomalies are not drawn from a single coherent distribution, and next month's anomalies won't resemble last month's. That means offline evaluation is fundamentally hard: you're modeling normality and flagging deviation, so the operating point is set by how many alerts your reviewers can actually process, not by a threshold that optimizes a score.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"How do you evaluate an anomaly detector with no labeled anomalies?"
"I'd use accuracy on the flagged points once we know which were real."
Without labels I'd use three complementary approaches. First, synthetic injection: perturb known-normal records in ways that mimic plausible anomalies and measure the detection rate — imperfect, since I'm testing against my own assumptions, but it gives a floor. Second, human-in-the-loop precision: sample the top-k flagged items, have analysts label them, and report precision@k, which is the number that matters operationally since reviewer capacity is the real constraint. Third, stability and coverage checks: does the flag rate stay roughly constant over time, and when it spikes is that a genuine event or a pipeline change? I'd also build the labeled set as I go — every analyst review is a label, so within a few months there's enough data to evaluate properly and possibly move to a supervised model for the anomaly types that recur. The trap to avoid is tuning the threshold to a fixed alert budget and then reporting that as accuracy.
Follow-up: "When would you switch from unsupervised to supervised here?"

Recommender Systems

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • Collaborative filtering learns from interaction patterns (matrix factorization decomposes the user-item matrix into latent factors; two-tower neural models generalize this to arbitrary features); content-based filtering matches item attributes to a user profile; production systems are hybrid and staged — cheap candidate generation over millions of items, then an expensive ranker over hundreds.
  • It matters because retrieve-then-rerank is the same architecture as RAG, and recsys solved it a decade earlier — the ANN index, the two-tower encoder, and the cross-encoder reranker are literally the same components.
  • The insight: the hard problems are cold start and feedback loops, not the model. A recommender trained on click logs learns from items it previously showed, so it reinforces its own past choices and the training data stops representing user preference — it represents past model behavior. Fixing this requires deliberate exploration (epsilon-greedy, bandits) and logging propensities so you can debias offline evaluation, which is the part teams skip.
▸ How this shows up in interviews
"How would you handle a brand-new user with no interaction history?"
"Show them the most popular items until we have enough data."
Popularity is the right fallback but it's the floor, not the plan. I'd layer three things. First, use whatever signal exists at signup — device, locale, referral source, and any onboarding preferences — through a content-based or feature-based model that doesn't need interaction history, which is exactly what a two-tower architecture gives you since the user tower consumes features rather than an ID embedding. Second, treat the first session as active exploration: deliberately diversify recommendations to gather information fast, since a contextual bandit framing makes the exploration/exploitation trade explicit rather than accidental. Third, transition smoothly — blend the content-based score with the collaborative score weighted by interaction count, so there's no discontinuity when the user crosses some threshold. I'd measure specifically on the new-user cohort, because aggregate metrics are dominated by established users and will hide cold-start failure entirely.
Follow-up: "How do you evaluate a recommender offline without deploying it?"

Time Series Forecasting

[TRADEOFF]
  • A series decomposes into trend, seasonality, and residual; classical models (ARIMA, exponential smoothing/ETS) model these explicitly and require stationarity, usually achieved by differencing; modern practice often reframes forecasting as supervised regression with lag and rolling-window features fed to a GBM, or uses deep models (N-BEATS, Temporal Fusion Transformer) when there are many related series.
  • It matters for demand planning, capacity forecasting, and — directly relevant to ML infrastructure — traffic and cost prediction for autoscaling.
  • The insight: every ordinary cross-validation habit is wrong here. Random k-fold shuffles future data into the training set, so the model interpolates rather than extrapolates and reports a score it can never achieve in production. Use rolling-origin (walk-forward) validation, and always compare against the seasonal naive baseline — "the value from the same weekday last week" beats a surprising number of sophisticated models, and any model that can't beat it isn't earning its maintenance cost.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"How do you validate a forecasting model?"
"Standard 80/20 train-test split with cross-validation."
Rolling-origin evaluation, sometimes called walk-forward. I train on data through time T, forecast the next h steps, score them, then advance T and repeat — which mirrors exactly how the model will be used and yields several fold scores so I can see variance across periods, not just an average. Random k-fold is disqualifying here because it trains on future points to predict past ones. I'd also fix the forecast horizon to the one the business actually needs, since accuracy at h=1 says almost nothing about h=30, and report the metric per horizon rather than pooled. For the metric, MASE or WAPE over MAPE, because MAPE is undefined at zero and asymmetric in a way that quietly biases models toward under-forecasting. And every result gets reported alongside the seasonal naive baseline — if the model doesn't beat it by a margin worth the operational complexity, the honest recommendation is to ship the baseline.

Interpretability: SHAP, LIME & Calibration

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • SHAP assigns each feature a contribution to an individual prediction using Shapley values from cooperative game theory, giving locally accurate and consistent attributions; LIME fits a simple interpretable model in the neighborhood of one prediction; calibration is separate — it asks whether a predicted 0.7 actually corresponds to a 70% empirical rate, checked with a reliability diagram and fixed with Platt scaling or isotonic regression.
  • It matters because regulated domains require explanations, because attributions are how you find leakage and bugs, and because downstream decisions that use probabilities as costs need those probabilities to be true.
  • The insight: feature attribution is not causal. SHAP tells you what the model used, not what drives the outcome — with correlated features, attribution splits arbitrarily among them, and removing a "high-SHAP" feature may not degrade the model at all because a correlate absorbs its role. Presenting SHAP values as causal claims to a business stakeholder is one of the fastest ways to lose credibility when the intervention they inspire does nothing.
Situation
What it costs you
Reading SHAP as causal
Interventions based on it fail; attribution ≠ effect

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Your churn model has great AUC, but the retention team says the discount offers it triggers don't work. What's your read?"
"The model needs retraining on more recent data."
Two distinct problems are likely, and neither is retraining. The first is calibration: AUC only measures ranking, so a model can order customers perfectly while its predicted probabilities are systematically off. If the team computes expected value of an offer using those probabilities, the whole budget allocation is wrong even though the ranking is right — I'd check a reliability diagram and fit isotonic regression on a held-out set if it's miscalibrated. The second and deeper problem is that predicting churn isn't the same as identifying who a discount would change. Some high-risk customers will leave regardless and some will stay regardless; spending on either is wasted. What they actually need is uplift modeling — estimating the treatment effect of the offer, which requires a randomized holdout to learn from. I'd propose running that holdout, because without it there's no data that can answer their question, no matter how good the churn model gets.
Follow-up: "How would you set up the holdout to measure uplift?"
