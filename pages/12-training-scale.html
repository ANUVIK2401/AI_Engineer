12 · Training & Scale

8 topics ~55 min deep-read Depth [CRITICAL]

Interviewers ask about this because training is where the money goes and where the subtle bugs live — optimizer state, precision, data quality, and context length are the levers that decide whether a run converges, and anyone who has actually trained a model can talk about them concretely.

Optimizers: SGD, Adam & AdamW

[CRITICAL]
  • SGD with momentum accumulates a velocity term to smooth noisy gradients; Adam adds per-parameter adaptive learning rates by tracking running estimates of the first moment (mean) and second moment (uncentered variance) of the gradient; AdamW fixes Adam's weight decay by decoupling it from the gradient update instead of folding it into the loss as L2.
  • They matter because optimizer choice determines whether a large model converges at all, and because Adam's optimizer state is a first-order term in your memory budget — not a detail.
  • The insight: Adam stores two extra fp32 tensors per parameter, so at fp32 the optimizer state is roughly 8 bytes per parameter on top of 4 for weights and 4 for gradients — meaning a 7B model needs about 112GB before a single activation is stored. That single fact is why ZeRO sharding, 8-bit optimizers, and LoRA exist, and why "the model fits in memory" is never the right calculation.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Why is AdamW the default for transformers rather than Adam or SGD?"
"AdamW converges faster because it adapts the learning rate per parameter."
Two separate things. Adam over SGD: transformer gradients vary enormously in scale across parameter groups — embeddings, attention projections, and layer norms have very different gradient magnitudes — so a single global learning rate either destabilizes some groups or starves others. Adam's per-parameter normalization by the second-moment estimate handles that automatically, which is what makes transformers trainable without extensive per-group tuning. AdamW over Adam is a distinct fix: in Adam, L2 regularization added to the loss gets divided by the same second-moment term as the gradient, so parameters with large gradients receive proportionally less decay — the regularization strength becomes an accident of gradient scale. AdamW decouples decay from the adaptive update and applies it directly to the weights, which restores the intended uniform regularization and empirically generalizes better. SGD with momentum still wins in some vision settings, where it often finds flatter minima, but for transformers the tuning cost is not worth it.

Learning Rate Schedules & Warmup

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • Standard transformer practice is linear warmup over the first small percentage of steps followed by cosine decay to near zero; alternatives include step decay, inverse-square-root (the original transformer schedule), and one-cycle. Gradient clipping by global norm usually accompanies it.
  • It matters because learning rate is the single most impactful hyperparameter, and the schedule interacts with batch size, model size, and total step budget — a schedule tuned for one run is often wrong for another.
  • The insight: warmup exists because Adam's second-moment estimate is unreliable in the first few hundred steps. With few samples, the variance estimate is noisy and can be very small, so dividing by its square root produces enormous effective steps that can destroy the initialization before training starts. Warmup gives the moment estimates time to stabilize. That's why warmup matters more for Adam than SGD, and more for large batches where each step is a bigger commitment.
▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Your loss spikes to NaN 200 steps into a fine-tuning run. What do you check?"
"Lower the learning rate and restart."
Lowering the LR often works but I'd rather know why, since the same bug will come back at scale. In order of likelihood: first, is there warmup? A NaN in the first few hundred steps is the signature of Adam's second-moment estimate being tiny and noisy early, producing an enormous effective step — warmup is the direct fix. Second, precision: pure fp16 has a narrow dynamic range and overflows in attention logits or layer norm; the fix is bf16, which has fp32's exponent range, or fp16 with a loss scaler that backs off on overflow. Third, gradient clipping — clip by global norm at 1.0, which bounds the damage from a single bad batch. Fourth, the data itself: I'd find the exact batch by logging the step index and replaying it, because a malformed sample, an empty sequence, or a sequence of all-padding producing a zero-denominator in a masked mean is a very common and very silent cause. I'd also check for a zero-length attention mask row, which NaNs the softmax deterministically.
Follow-up: "Why does bf16 avoid the overflow that fp16 hits?"

Mixed Precision & Numerical Stability

[TRADEOFF]
  • fp16 has 10 mantissa bits and 5 exponent bits — high precision, narrow range; bf16 has 7 mantissa and 8 exponent bits — same range as fp32, less precision. Mixed precision keeps an fp32 master copy of weights and optimizer state while running forward and backward in 16-bit, with loss scaling for fp16 to keep small gradients from flushing to zero.
  • It matters because 16-bit training roughly halves memory and doubles throughput on tensor-core hardware, which is the difference between a run being feasible and not.
  • The insight: range matters more than precision for training. Gradients span many orders of magnitude, so fp16 overflows at the top and underflows at the bottom — hence loss scaling, which is a workaround for insufficient exponent bits. bf16 has fp32's exponent range and simply doesn't need loss scaling, which is why it became the default on hardware that supports it despite having fewer mantissa bits.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Why keep an fp32 master copy of weights if you're training in bf16?"
"For safety, in case the 16-bit weights get corrupted."
Because of update magnitude relative to representable precision. Late in training the weight updates are tiny — the learning rate has decayed and gradients are small — and bf16 has only 7 mantissa bits, roughly 2–3 decimal digits. When you add an update that is many orders of magnitude smaller than the weight, bf16 rounds it away entirely and the update is silently lost. Training appears to just stop improving, with no error. Keeping the master weights and optimizer moments in fp32 means updates accumulate at full precision, and you cast down to bf16 only for the forward and backward passes where the extra precision genuinely doesn't matter. This is also why the optimizer state dominates the memory budget and why 8-bit optimizers use block-wise quantization with careful outlier handling rather than naive casting — the same silent-rounding failure applies to the moment estimates.

Distributed Training: DDP, ZeRO & FSDP

[CRITICAL]
  • Data parallelism replicates the model on every GPU and all-reduces gradients; ZeRO/FSDP shards optimizer state (stage 1), gradients (stage 2), and parameters (stage 3) across devices, gathering each layer's weights just in time and freeing them after. Tensor parallelism splits individual matrices across GPUs; pipeline parallelism assigns different layers to different devices.
  • They matter because past a few billion parameters no single device holds the training state, and picking the wrong parallelism strategy wastes most of a cluster on communication.
  • The insight: each strategy trades memory for a different communication pattern, and the right choice follows the interconnect. Tensor parallelism communicates inside every layer, so it demands NVLink-class bandwidth and stays within a node. Pipeline parallelism communicates only at stage boundaries, so it tolerates slower links across nodes but introduces bubble overhead. FSDP sits between them. Real frontier runs combine all three — that's the "3D parallelism" you'll be asked about.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"You have 8 A100s (80GB each) and want to fine-tune a 70B model. Is it possible?"
"No — 70B needs 140GB in fp16, which exceeds a single GPU."
Full fine-tuning, no; parameter-efficient fine-tuning, comfortably yes. Full fine-tuning needs roughly 16 bytes per parameter with Adam in mixed precision — about 1.1TB — against 640GB of aggregate VRAM, and that's before activations. Even ZeRO-3 sharding across 8 GPUs doesn't close a gap that size, and CPU offload would make it unusably slow. QLoRA is the practical route: quantize the base model to 4-bit, which puts the frozen weights at roughly 35GB and lets them shard easily, and train only low-rank adapters. Optimizer state now covers a tiny fraction of parameters, so the whole thing fits with room for a reasonable batch and sequence length. The tradeoffs to state honestly are that 4-bit quantization costs some quality, LoRA can't make distributional shifts that exceed the rank of the update, and inference then requires either keeping the adapter separate or merging it back. For most fine-tuning goals — style, format, domain adaptation — that's entirely sufficient, and I'd only argue for full fine-tuning if evaluation showed the adapter genuinely capping quality.

Scaling Laws & Compute Budgets

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • Loss falls as a power law in model parameters, dataset tokens, and compute. The Chinchilla result showed that for a fixed compute budget, earlier models were badly oversized relative to their data — the compute-optimal ratio is roughly 20 tokens per parameter, not the far lower ratios used before it.
  • They matter because they turn "how big should the model be" from a guess into an estimate, and because they let you predict a large run's loss from a ladder of small ones before spending the budget.
  • The insight: compute-optimal is not deployment-optimal. Chinchilla minimizes training loss per unit of training compute, but a deployed model's cost is dominated by inference over its lifetime. If you serve billions of tokens, it's rational to train a smaller model far past its compute-optimal token count — spending more training compute for a permanently cheaper model. That's exactly why modern open models are trained on token counts many times the Chinchilla ratio.
$$ L(N, D) \approx L_\infty + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} \qquad C \approx 6ND $$

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"You have a fixed training budget. Bigger model or more data?"
"Bigger model — parameter count is what drives capability."
For pure compute-optimality, Chinchilla says balance them at roughly 20 tokens per parameter, and the pre-Chinchilla instinct to maximize parameters was measurably wrong. But I'd push back on the framing, because the right answer depends on what happens after training. If this model will serve high volume, I'd deliberately train smaller and longer than compute-optimal — training compute is paid once and inference compute is paid forever, so a model that's 30% worse per training FLOP but half the size can be a large net win. If it's a research artifact or will serve little traffic, compute-optimal is right. Two caveats I'd raise regardless: the scaling law assumes unique high-quality tokens, and if I'd have to repeat data several epochs to reach the token target, returns fall off sharply and I should spend that budget on data curation instead. And the laws predict loss, not capabilities — the specific downstream behavior I care about may not track loss smoothly, so I'd validate with a ladder of small runs before committing.

Training Data: Curation & Contamination

[CRITICAL]
  • The pretraining data pipeline is quality filtering (classifier or heuristic), deduplication (exact hashing plus near-duplicate MinHash/LSH), decontamination against evaluation sets, PII and toxicity filtering, and mixture weighting across domains. For fine-tuning, the same logic applies at much smaller scale with far higher per-example standards.
  • It matters because at fixed compute, data quality moves benchmarks more than architecture does — and because a contaminated eval set makes every subsequent decision unreliable.
  • The insight: deduplication is not a cleanliness nicety, it changes what the model learns. Duplicated documents get effectively upweighted, increasing memorization and verbatim regurgitation while wasting compute on repeated content — and near-duplicates are the majority of the problem, so exact hashing alone misses most of it. For instruction tuning, the well-replicated finding is that a few thousand carefully curated examples beat hundreds of thousands of scraped ones.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Your fine-tuned model scores 91% on your benchmark but users report it's worse than the base model. What happened?"
"The benchmark must be too easy — I'd find a harder one."
Two likely causes, and I'd check contamination first because it's cheap to test. If benchmark examples leaked into the fine-tuning set — directly, or through a synthetic-data generator that saw them — the score measures memorization, not capability. I'd run n-gram overlap between the training data and the eval set, and separately check whether the model reproduces eval items verbatim when prompted with a prefix, which is fairly conclusive. The second cause is catastrophic forgetting or alignment tax: fine-tuning on a narrow distribution degrades general capability and often instruction-following, so the model gets better at the benchmark's shape and worse at everything users actually do. The diagnostic is to evaluate on a held-out general benchmark the model was never tuned toward, plus a sample of real production prompts, and compare against the base model directly. Mitigations are mixing a fraction of general instruction data into the fine-tuning mixture, lowering the learning rate, using LoRA so the base weights are preserved, and early-stopping on the general eval rather than the task eval.

Long Context: RoPE Scaling & Efficient Attention

[TRADEOFF]
  • Extending context requires two things: positional encodings that generalize beyond the trained length (RoPE with position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, or YaRN) and attention that doesn't blow up quadratically. FlashAttention keeps exact attention but tiles the computation to avoid materializing the n×n matrix in HBM; sliding-window and sparse attention change the computation itself.
  • It matters because context length is a headline product feature, and because the difference between "supports 200k tokens" and "uses 200k tokens well" is where systems quietly fail.
  • The insight: FlashAttention is not an approximation — it computes exactly the same attention, just with an IO-aware algorithm that avoids writing the score matrix to slow memory. That distinction matters in interviews: people often lump it with sparse attention, but one is a memory-access optimization with identical outputs and the other is a modeling approximation with different outputs. Separately, long context degrades quality in the middle of the window ("lost in the middle"), so retrieval into a shorter context often beats stuffing everything in.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Models support 1M-token context now. Does that make RAG obsolete?"
"Mostly yes — you can just put all the documents in the prompt."
No, for three reasons that are independent of model capability. Cost and latency: prefill is linear in tokens, so putting a million tokens in every request is orders of magnitude more expensive than retrieving the few thousand that matter, and it adds seconds of time-to-first-token on every call. Quality: the lost-in-the-middle effect is well documented — retrieval accuracy dips for content in the middle of a long window, so more context does not monotonically improve answers and can actively hurt. Attribution and freshness: RAG returns which documents were used, which is what makes citation, permissions filtering, and auditability possible, and the index updates without touching the model. Where long context genuinely wins is when the task requires global reasoning over one coherent document — summarizing a whole contract, or tracing a change through a codebase — because chunking destroys exactly the cross-references those tasks need. The mature answer is that they compose: retrieve aggressively, then use the long window to hold a generous amount of retrieved context rather than a stingy top-3.

Debugging a Training Run

[FREQUENTLY ASKED]
  • The standard instrumentation is training and validation loss, gradient norm before clipping, learning rate, parameter update-to-weight ratio, activation statistics per layer, and throughput in tokens per second. The first diagnostic move is always to overfit a single batch — if the model can't drive loss to near zero on 8 examples, the bug is in the code, not the hyperparameters.
  • It matters because training failures are expensive and mostly silent: the loss curve looks plausible while something specific and fixable is wrong.
  • The insight: the update-to-weight ratio is the most underused signal available. Roughly 1e-3 per step is healthy — much smaller means the model is effectively frozen regardless of what the LR says, much larger means it's being destroyed. It catches problems the loss curve hides, like a learning rate that's fine globally but wrong for one parameter group, and it's a single scalar per group you can log for free.

▸ How this shows up in interviews
"Training loss is decreasing normally but the model produces garbage at inference. What's wrong?"
"It's underfitting — I'd train for more epochs."
A decreasing training loss with broken generation almost always means a train/inference mismatch rather than a modeling problem, so I'd look at the seams between the two code paths. The usual suspects, in order: the tokenizer or chat template differs between training and inference — trained with one format, prompted with another, so the model never sees the format it's being asked to complete; the loss is being computed over the prompt tokens as well as the completion, when it should be masked to the completion only; padding side is wrong — right-padding at inference for a decoder means generation starts after pad tokens; special tokens like BOS or EOS added at training and not at inference or vice versa; or the model is being loaded without the adapter merged or with a dtype mismatch. The decisive test is to take one training example, run it through the exact inference path, and compare the tokenized input ids byte-for-byte with what the training dataloader produced. That comparison finds the bug almost every time and takes ten minutes.
